Executive Summary

The strong sulfur smell at your property is caused by hydrogen sulfide gas being produced by sulfate-reducing bacteria that have colonized your water system. Testing confirms this contamination is present at every stage — from the spring source through the cisterns and storage tank. Based on the findings, the odor is likely addressable. If the bacterial contamination is successfully eliminated through a system-wide remediation program, the smell should resolve.

While you came to us about the smell, our testing also identified an important related finding: copper in your drinking water is above the EPA action level (1.42 mg/L vs. limit of 1.3 mg/L). The most probable cause is the same bacterial contamination — hydrogen sulfide is likely corroding your copper pipes and dissolving copper into the water. Addressing the smell and the copper are most likely the same fix.

The contamination begins before the cisterns. The spring source itself tested positive for sulfate-reducing bacteria at 6,000 CFU/mL, meaning remediation must address the entire system — spring intake, cisterns, and storage tank — not just downstream components.

Root Cause: One Chain, Six Steps
The sulfur smell, the blue-black pipes, and the elevated copper are not separate problems — they are one causal chain that starts at the spring source.
Step01

The contamination begins at the spring.

Spring source water contains active sulfate-reducing bacteria at 6,000 CFU/mL. This is the origin point. Any remediation that stops at the cisterns will be re-seeded from the source.

Step02

Bacteria colonize the cisterns and storage tank.

SRB and associated iron-related and slime-forming bacteria colonize downstream storage, forming biofilm on tank walls. The storage tank now measures 570,000 CFU/mL iron-related bacteria.

Step03

The smell — SRB produce hydrogen sulfide.

SRB metabolize sulfate compounds and produce hydrogen sulfide (H₂S) — a gas that smells like rotten eggs. This gas releases into the air every time a faucet is opened or during a shower.

Step04

H₂S reacts with the copper pipes.

Hydrogen sulfide reacts chemically with copper to form copper sulfide — the blue-black coating visible in the mechanical room. This pattern is consistent with active corrosion rather than cosmetic discoloration.

Step05

Corroding pipes release copper into the water.

As the pipe walls corrode, copper dissolves into the water supply. This is the most probable explanation for copper at 1.42 mg/L at the kitchen sink — above the EPA action level of 1.30 mg/L.

Step06

Soft, mineral-poor water accelerates corrosion.

The water carries little protective mineral content. With no calcium scale forming on pipe walls, the corrosion cycle has nothing to slow it down — biology and chemistry compound each other.

Spring Source — Addressing the Open Inlet
The open-air entry point between the mountain outlet and the cistern is a likely contamination pathway. Four options exist for closing it, in order of effectiveness.
The spring water at Sunshine Ranch flows from a pipe outlet in the mountain and drops openly into the cistern. This open-air entry point is a likely contamination pathway — exposing the water to oxygen, insects, surface runoff, and soil bacteria before it enters the storage system. Addressing this is an important part of the long-term remediation.
Recommended Options — In Order of Effectiveness
01

Spring box

A sealed concrete or HDPE enclosure built around the spring outlet. Water flows into the sealed box rather than open air, preventing contamination from the environment. This is the industry standard for mountain spring systems and the most effective long-term solution. A licensed water system contractor or well driller can design and install one appropriate for this site.

Recommended
02

Sealed intake cover

If a full spring box is not immediately feasible, a sealed pipe cap with a screened vent at the outlet point significantly reduces open-air exposure. Not a permanent fix but meaningfully better than an unprotected open drop.

Interim
03

UV disinfection at cistern entry

A UV treatment unit installed where water enters the cistern kills bacteria before they enter the storage system. Does not address the source contamination but provides an important layer of protection downstream while longer-term fixes are implemented.

Ongoing Treatment
04

Continuous chlorination at cistern inlet

Automated chlorine dosing at the point where spring water enters the cistern keeps bacterial counts suppressed on an ongoing basis. Standard practice for spring-fed systems in Colorado mountain properties.

Ongoing Treatment
Note: Items 3 and 4 are ongoing treatment measures, not source fixes. The spring box (item 1) is the only solution that addresses the contamination entry point directly. All four measures can be used in combination for maximum protection.
Ongoing Monitoring Program
InHaus Lab recommends the following testing schedule following remediation — designed to catch any SRB regrowth early, before it has a chance to re-establish as biofilm.
Step 01

60–90 days post-remediation

Full system retest to confirm shock treatment was successful and bacterial counts are at zero.

Step 02 · Year 1

Monthly for the first year

SRB and biofilm bacteria testing at the spring source, cistern, and storage tank. Catching early regrowth means a simple re-treatment rather than a full shock disinfection program.

Step 03 · Ongoing

Quarterly ongoing

Once the system has demonstrated 12 months of clean results, quarterly testing is generally sufficient.

Why This Matters

Sulfate-reducing bacteria can begin re-colonizing a system within weeks under the right conditions — particularly in spring-fed cisterns where source water is constantly introducing new biological material. Monthly testing is the early warning system that keeps a small problem from becoming a large one.
