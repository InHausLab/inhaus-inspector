Professor Emeritus of Medicine and Public Health

Dr. Stephen J. Jay, MD

Indiana University School of Medicine  ·  Richard M. Fairbanks School of Public Health  ·  Indianapolis, Indiana
Dr. Stephen Jordan Jay, MD is one of the most consequential figures in Indiana public health over the last half century — a bedside pulmonologist for over 60 years, the founding chair of Indiana's first school of public health, a policy advocate who built the state's tobacco control infrastructure, and a medical historian who rescued from obscurity the Hoosier physician who first warned America about smoking and lung cancer. His PubMed-indexed record spans 104 papers across 40 years. Over his career he worked alongside some of the most significant figures in American public health, environmental policy, and anti-tobacco advocacy.

Education and Training

Jay attended Wabash College in Crawfordsville, Indiana — a small, all-male liberal arts college with a strong tradition of producing physicians — before entering medical school. He graduated from the Indiana University School of Medicine (IUSM) in 1966 as an Alpha Omega Alpha honor graduate, the highest academic distinction in American medical education, reserved for approximately the top 10 percent of the class.

He then completed graduate medical education in internal medicine and pulmonary medicine at the University of Texas Southwestern Medical School and Parkland Memorial Hospital in Dallas — one of the country's most rigorous clinical training environments. He returned to Indiana and joined the IUSM faculty in 1976.

Clinical Career

Jay practiced as an internal medicine physician and pulmonologist for over 60 years, placing him among the longest-serving physicians in Indiana history. His clinical specialties were pulmonary disease and pleural disease. He was affiliated with Methodist Hospital, Wishard Memorial Hospital (now Eskenazi Health), and Indiana University Health Hospital, all in Indianapolis. He remained board certified in Internal Medicine and licensed to practice in Indiana as of the most recent available records.

Academic Career at Indiana University

  • Head, Division of Pulmonology, IUSM — overseeing clinical and academic pulmonary medicine at IU
  • Dean for Continuing Medical Education, IUSM (1994–1999) — responsible for accreditation and delivery of professional education for practicing physicians across Indiana
  • Founding Chair, IU Richard M. Fairbanks School of Public Health (1999–2006) — built Indiana's first standalone school of public health from the ground up; recruited faculty, established curricula, secured accreditation
  • Professor Emeritus of Medicine and Public Health — current title
  • Director, Indiana's first HRSA-funded Area Health Education Center (AHEC) — trained health professionals to serve underserved rural and urban communities

The Fairbanks School, which Jay built from nothing, is now a fully accredited degree-granting school offering MPH, PhD, and doctoral programs with research centers in cancer prevention, health policy, epidemiology, and environmental health.

Teaching Recognition

Jay received teaching excellence awards from the University of Kentucky School of Medicine and from Indiana University School of Medicine in both 1998 and 2003. In 2016 he received the IU School of Medicine Glenn W. Irwin, Jr. Distinguished Faculty Award, given to educators demonstrating "a passion for educating students, collaborating with peers, and pushing the boundaries of research." The citation called him "a pioneer in the field of public health" best known for smoking cessation advocacy.

Cluster 1 — Tobacco Control and Public Health Policy
"A Decade of Sustaining Best Practices for Tobacco Control: Indiana's Story"
Jay SJ, Torabi MR, Spitznagle MH. Preventing Chronic Disease, 2012;9:E37.
PMID: 22239752 — Free PMC Article
His most substantial policy paper. Chronicles the creation of the Indiana Tobacco Prevention and Cessation Agency (ITPC) in 2000 through its abolition by the Indiana legislature in April 2011. Key findings: when ITPC was created, 27% of Indiana adults smoked vs. the national median of 23%. By 2010, tobacco use caused 9,700 deaths annually in Indiana and $4.7 billion in health and economic costs, including $487 million in Medicaid payments. Indiana ranked 49th in per-capita public health spending and ran ITPC at less than 20% of the CDC-recommended funding level. Despite this, ITPC measurably reduced smoking rates and increased smoke-free environments.

Expert Witness — Tobacco Litigation

The Tobacco Industry Was Watching Him.

The UCSF Truth Tobacco Industry Documents Library — the archive of over 90 million pages of previously secret tobacco industry records made public under the Master Settlement Agreement — contains 209 separate documents referencing Dr. Stephen Jay.

That number is not an accident. The tobacco industry maintained active dossiers on physicians and researchers they considered credible threats to their legal defenses and public credibility. 209 documents means RJ Reynolds and its lawyers were tracking Jay's work, his testimony, his publications, and his public statements — systematically, over years.

Among those documents: an internal RJ Reynolds litigation memo titled "Tobacco Industry — Rogers Case — Plaintiffs Witnesses", dated February 9, 1995 — two days after Jay finished testifying against them in court. The industry's own lawyers were writing internal assessments of his testimony in real time. That is what it looks like when an Indianapolis physician makes the tobacco industry nervous.

Rogers vs. R.J. Reynolds Tobacco Co. — Trial Testimony of Stephen Jay
February 3 and 7, 1995. Four volumes, four sessions. 534 pages of trial transcript. UCSF Industry Documents Library, RJ Reynolds Records / Master Settlement Agreement collection. Document IDs: jnkp0061, knkp0061, xnkp0061, lnkp0061. Publicly available, no restrictions.
Dr. Jay testified as an expert witness for the plaintiffs in Rogers vs. R.J. Reynolds Tobacco Co. — one of the major tobacco personal injury cases of the 1990s that preceded and contributed to the $246 billion national Master Settlement Agreement. He testified over two full days (February 3 and 7, 1995), across four sessions (morning and afternoon each day), producing 534 pages of sworn trial transcript. As a pulmonologist with decades of clinical experience treating lung disease, and as Indiana's leading public health voice on tobacco, he was exactly the kind of credible, senior medical expert that tobacco plaintiffs' attorneys sought — and that the industry feared.

RJ Reynolds was required to produce and preserve his full testimony as part of the Minnesota and Florida multi-state litigation document requests. The transcripts are now permanently in the public record, freely accessible in the UCSF Truth Tobacco Industry Documents Library. They sit alongside internal industry strategy memos — including the February 9, 1995 memo assessing Jay specifically as a plaintiff's witness — that document how seriously the industry took him as an adversary.
What it meant to be an expert witness against Big Tobacco in 1995: The tobacco industry in the mid-1990s had virtually unlimited legal resources, employed hundreds of lawyers, and had spent decades building strategies to discredit, intimidate, and professionally isolate physicians and researchers who testified against them. Expert witnesses faced aggressive deposition tactics, attacks on their credentials, and in some cases professional retaliation. Jay testified anyway — two full days, on the record, under oath. The tobacco industry's own documents confirm they considered him a serious enough threat to warrant active monitoring before, during, and after his testimony.

Notable Colleagues and Allies

Over his career, Dr. Jay worked alongside and intersected with some of the most significant figures in American public health, environmental policy, and the fight against Big Tobacco.

Janet McCabe

Deputy Administrator, U.S. Environmental Protection Agency (2021–2024) · IU Environmental Resilience Institute

Janet McCabe is one of the most senior environmental regulators in recent American history. Before rising to the No. 2 position at the EPA under President Biden — confirmed by the Senate in April 2021 — she was executive director of Improving Kids' Environment, an Indianapolis children's environmental health nonprofit. Dr. Jay held a leadership role with the same organization. McCabe also served as Assistant Commissioner of the Indiana Department of Environmental Management's Office of Air Quality (1999–2005) and as Acting Assistant Administrator of the EPA's Office of Air and Radiation under Obama (2013–2017). After her EPA tenure she returned to IU as a Visiting Professor and Executive in Residence at the O'Neill School of Public and Environmental Affairs.

Connection to Dr. Jay: Both held leadership roles with Improving Kids' Environment in Indianapolis and operated within the same Indiana public health and environmental advocacy community. McCabe's work on air quality and environmental health overlapped directly with Jay's public health infrastructure work at the Fairbanks School.

Senator Richard G. Lugar (R-Indiana)

U.S. Senator, Indiana, 1977–2013 · Mayor of Indianapolis, 1968–1975

Richard Lugar was one of the most respected and longest-serving members of the United States Senate, representing Indiana for 36 years. Though a conservative Republican, Lugar was a genuine bipartisan voice on public health. He was a co-champion of the Comprehensive Smokeless Tobacco Education Act of 1986 — working alongside Representative Henry Waxman and a bipartisan coalition to mandate health warnings on smokeless tobacco products and ban their advertising on radio and television. This made him one of the few Senate Republicans willing to take on the tobacco industry at its height. He also co-sponsored legislation related to the FDA's authority over tobacco products and was part of the Senate coalition that enabled the Master Settlement Agreement framework. Lugar served as chairman of the Senate Foreign Relations Committee and was a foundational figure in Indiana civic and public life for half a century.

Dr. Jay testified before Lugar's committee in Washington, D.C. on February 3, 2000 — the U.S. Senate Committee on Agriculture, Nutrition, and Forestry, chaired by Lugar, hearing on Rural Satellite and Cable Systems Loan Guarantees and the Digital Divide in Rural America (Senate Hearing 106-697, Russell Senate Office Building, Washington D.C.). Jay appeared as a named witness in his capacity as Assistant Dean for Continuing Medical Education and Chairman of the Department of Public Health at Indiana University School of Medicine. His prepared statement is entered into the permanent Congressional record at page 90 of the hearing transcript. His testimony addressed how rural communities lacked access to the telemedicine, distance learning, and health information infrastructure available in urban areas — and the role broadband connectivity could play in closing that gap. Senator Lugar presided over the hearing.

Documented connection: Jay and Lugar were in the same Senate hearing room on February 3, 2000 — Lugar as chairman, Jay as expert witness. Their collaboration spanned both the legislative (Lugar's tobacco bills) and the technology-access-to-healthcare policy that Jay championed from the public health side. Source: S. Hrg. 106-697, U.S. Government Publishing Office.

Stanton Glantz, PhD

Professor of Medicine, UCSF · Founding Director, Center for Tobacco Control Research and Education · American Legacy Foundation Distinguished Professor of Tobacco Control

Stanton Glantz is arguably the most consequential anti-tobacco researcher in American history. A professor of medicine and cardiology at the University of California, San Francisco, he has spent five decades dismantling the tobacco industry's scientific and political defenses. His defining moment came in May 1994 when an anonymous FedEx package arrived at his office — return address simply "Mr. Butts" — containing over 4,000 pages of secret internal documents from Brown & Williamson tobacco company. The documents proved that the tobacco industry had known since 1963 that nicotine was addictive and smoking caused cancer, while publicly denying both. Glantz and his colleagues published five groundbreaking papers based on the documents in the Journal of the American Medical Association and posted the source documents online — the origin of what became the UCSF Truth Tobacco Industry Documents Library, now containing over 90 million pages of previously secret tobacco industry records freely available to the world.

His research on secondhand smoke demonstrated that its cardiovascular effects are nearly as large as smoking itself — a finding that provided the scientific foundation for smoke-free workplace laws nationwide. A 1991 study showed secondhand smoke causes heart disease. A later study demonstrated that smoke-free laws led to as many as 20% fewer heart attacks. The tobacco industry sued the University of California twice — unsuccessfully — to stop his work.

Glantz served as founding director of the UCSF Center for Tobacco Control Research and Education for 20 years, led a $40 million FDA/NIH Tobacco Centers of Regulatory Science program, and ran the TobaccoScam project that broke the alliance between the tobacco and hospitality industries. He has been a globally recognized expert on e-cigarettes and has applied his tobacco industry expertise to the sugar and marijuana industries as well.

Connection to Dr. Jay: Glantz and Jay operated as parallel pillars of the American tobacco control movement — Glantz producing the foundational research and exposing industry documents on the national and international stage; Jay translating that science into Indiana state policy, building the institutional infrastructure to deliver tobacco control programs, and fighting the political battles in Indiana that Glantz fought in California. Both men's careers were defined by confronting an industry with enormous political power using evidence, persistence, and institutional credibility.

Summary

Dr. Stephen J. Jay is one of the most consequential figures in Indiana public health over the last half century. He operated across four distinct domains simultaneously: as a bedside pulmonologist for over 60 years; as an institution-builder who founded Indiana's first school of public health from scratch; as a policy advocate who built and defended the state's tobacco control infrastructure; and as a medical historian who rescued from obscurity the Hoosier physician who first warned America about smoking and lung cancer.

His publication record of 104 indexed papers spanning 40 years covers tuberculosis, tobacco, hypertension, cardiovascular economics, and the history of American dentistry. His network reached from the halls of the U.S. Senate to the EPA, from the foremost anti-tobacco researcher in America to the world stage. His awards span the clinical, academic, policy, and humanistic dimensions of his career in equal measure. He is, in the fullest sense, a complete physician in the old tradition of the term.
